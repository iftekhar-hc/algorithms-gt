## 1. Executive Summary

This lecture introduces **Competitive Analysis**—the standard framework for analyzing **Online Algorithms** where input arrives sequentially and irrevocable decisions must be made at each step—through three canonical problems:
1. **Online Makespan Scheduling (Graham's List Scheduling, 1966):** Assigning arriving jobs of duration $p_j$ to the currently least-loaded of $m$ identical machines achieves a competitive ratio of **$2 - \frac{1}{m}$** (proven via two simple lower bounds on the offline optimum $\text{OPT}$).
2. **Online Metric Steiner Tree:** Connecting arriving terminals $t_1, \dots, t_k$ greedily to the nearest previously arrived terminal achieves a competitive ratio of **$2 H_{k-1} \le 2 \ln k = O(\log k)$** (proven via the **Eulerian Tree-Doubling Trick**), which is asymptotically optimal among all online algorithms.
3. **Introduction to Online Bipartite Matching & Web Display Advertising:** Setting up the online ad-allocation model analyzed in Lecture 14.

## 2. Case Study 1: Online Makespan Scheduling (Graham's Algorithm)

### 2.1 Problem Setup
- **Known Up Front:** $m$ identical parallel machines $\{1, \dots, m\}$.
- **Arriving Online:** Jobs $j = 1, 2, \dots, n$ arrive one by one, each revealing its processing time $p_j \ge 0$ upon arrival.
- **Irrevocable Online Decision:** Immediately assign job $j$ to one machine $i \in \{1, \dots, m\}$.
- **Objective (Minimize Makespan):** Let $L_i = \sum_{j \text{ assigned to } i} p_j$ be the total load on machine $i$. Minimize the **makespan** (completion time of the last machine to finish):
  $$\text{Makespan} = \max_{1 \le i \le m} L_i$$

### 2.2 Competitive Ratio Definition
An online minimization algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ is **$\alpha$-competitive** (for $\alpha \ge 1$) if for **every** input sequence $I$,
$$\text{Cost}(\mathcal{A}(I)) \le \alpha \cdot \text{OPT}(I)$$
where $\text{OPT}(I)$ is the cost of the **optimal offline solution in hindsight** (computed with full foreknowledge of the entire input sequence $I$, and unlimited computational power).

### 2.3 Graham's Greedy List Scheduling Algorithm (1966)
> **Algorithm:** When job $j$ arrives, assign it to the machine with the **smallest current load**:
> $$i^* = \arg\min_{1 \le i \le m} L_i^{(j-1)}$$

```text
  Machine 1: [====== L_1 ======]
  Machine 2: [==== L_2 ====][---- j* (last job to finish, duration p_{j*}) ----]  <-- Max Load L_{i*}
  Machine 3: [======= L_3 =======]
                            ^
                            |-- Load S just before j* arrived: EVERY machine had load >= S!
```

### 2.4 Proof of $\left(2 - \frac{1}{m}\right)$-Competitiveness
> **Theorem (Graham, 1966):** Graham's Greedy Online Scheduling Algorithm achieves makespan at most $\left(2 - \frac{1}{m}\right) \text{OPT}$.

**Proof via Two Lower Bounds on $\text{OPT}$:**
How can we compare our online schedule against $\text{OPT}$ when $\text{OPT}$ is NP-hard even offline? We establish two universal lower bounds on $\text{OPT}$:
1. **Lower Bound 1 (Max Job Size):** Even in $\text{OPT}$, every job $j$ must be scheduled on *some* machine, so:
   $$\text{OPT} \ge \max_{1 \le j \le n} p_j$$
2. **Lower Bound 2 (Average Machine Load):** The sum of all $m$ machine loads equals $\sum_{j=1}^n p_j$, and the maximum of $m$ numbers is at least their average, so:
   $$\text{OPT} \ge \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^n p_j$$

**Analyzing the Bottleneck Machine $i^*$:**
Let $i^*$ be the machine with the maximum final load in Graham's schedule, and let $j^*$ be the **last job assigned to machine $i^*$**. Let $S$ be the load on machine $i^*$ **just before job $j^*$ was assigned to it** (so the final makespan is $L_{i^*} = S + p_{j^*}$).
- Because Graham's rule assigned $j^*$ to the machine with the **minimum** load at that moment, **every machine $i \in \{1, \dots, m\}$ had load at least $S$** when $j^*$ arrived (and loads only increase thereafter).
- Summing final loads across all $m$ machines (and separating out $j^*$'s duration $p_{j^*}$ on machine $i^*$):
  $$\sum_{j=1}^n p_j = \sum_{i=1}^m L_i \ge m \cdot S + p_{j^*} \quad \implies \quad S \le \frac{1}{m}\left(\sum_{j=1}^n p_j - p_{j^*}\right)$$
- Adding $p_{j^*}$ to both sides gives the exact makespan bound:
  $$\text{Makespan} = S + p_{j^*} \le \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^n p_j + \left(1 - \frac{1}{m}\right) p_{j^*} \le \text{OPT} + \left(1 - \frac{1}{m}\right)\text{OPT} = \left(2 - \frac{1}{m}\right)\text{OPT} \quad \blacksquare$$

*(Note: This bound is **tight**—consider $m(m-1)$ tiny jobs of size $1$ followed by one big job of size $m$: Greedy balances the tiny jobs to load $m-1$ on every machine and puts the size-$m$ job on one machine for makespan $2m - 1$, whereas $\text{OPT} = m$, giving ratio $\frac{2m-1}{m} = 2 - \frac{1}{m}$.)*

## 3. Case Study 2: The Online Metric Steiner Tree Problem

### 3.1 Problem Statement & Metric Reduction
- **Known Up Front:** An undirected graph $G = (V, E)$ with non-negative edge costs $c_e \ge 0$.
  - **WLOG Metric Assumption:** By replacing $G$ with its metric completion (where $c_{uv}$ is the shortest-path distance between $u$ and $v$ in $G$), we may assume $G$ is a complete graph satisfying the **Triangle Inequality**:
    $$c_{uw} \le c_{uv} + c_{vw} \qquad \forall u, v, w \in V$$
- **Arriving Online:** A sequence of **terminals** $t_1, t_2, \dots, t_k \in V$ arrives one by one.
- **Irrevocable Online Decision:** Maintain a tree $T \subseteq E$ such that after $t_i$ arrives, $T$ spans $\{t_1, \dots, t_i\}$.
- **Offline Benchmark ($\text{OPT}$):** The minimum-cost **Steiner Tree** $T^*$ in $G$ spanning $\{t_1, \dots, t_k\}$ (which is allowed to use non-terminal "Steiner vertices" $V \setminus \{t_1, \dots, t_k\}$ as intermediate hubs).

### 3.2 The Online Greedy Algorithm
> **Algorithm:** Initialize $T = \emptyset$ when $t_1$ arrives. For $i = 2, \dots, k$, when terminal $t_i$ arrives, add to $T$ the **cheapest direct edge connecting $t_i$ to any previously arrived terminal** $\{t_1, \dots, t_{i-1}\}$:
> $$e_i = \arg\min_{1 \le j < i} c(t_i, t_j)$$

### 3.3 Competitive Analysis ($2 H_{k-1} \le 2 \ln k$) via the Eulerian Tree-Doubling Trick
> **Theorem:** The Online Greedy Algorithm is **$2 \ln k$-competitive** for Online Steiner Tree (and no online algorithm, deterministic or randomized, can beat $\Omega(\log k)$).

This follows immediately from summing the following **Key Lemma** over $r = 1, \dots, k - 1$ ($\sum_{r=1}^{k-1} \frac{2\text{OPT}}{r} = 2 H_{k-1} \text{OPT} \le 2 \ln k \cdot \text{OPT}$):

> **Key Lemma:** For every $r \in \{1, 2, \dots, k - 1\}$, the **$r$-th most expensive edge** chosen by the Online Greedy Algorithm has cost at most $\frac{2 \cdot \text{OPT}}{r}$.

**Proof of the Key Lemma (Eulerian Tree-Doubling + Pigeonhole):**

```text
   Step 1: Optimal Steiner Tree T*        Step 2: Double every edge of T* to get H
           (Cost = OPT)                           (Eulerian Tour C of cost 2*OPT)
                (s)                                     (s)
              /  |  \                                //  ||  \\
             1   1   1                              (t1) (t2) (t3)
            /    |    \                  Tour C visits all terminals in circular order;
          (t1)  (t2)  (t3)               r terminals partition C into r edge-disjoint arcs!
```

1. **Double the Optimal Steiner Tree $T^*$:** Let $T^*$ be the offline optimal Steiner tree (cost $\text{OPT}$), and form multigraph $H$ by taking **two parallel copies** of every edge in $T^*$.
   - Total cost of $H$ is $c(H) = 2 \cdot \text{OPT}$.
   - Every vertex in $H$ has **even degree**, and $H$ is connected, so $H$ has an **Eulerian Tour $C$** (a closed walk traversing every edge of $H$ exactly once) of total cost $2 \cdot \text{OPT}$.
2. **Partition the Eulerian Tour $C$ Using the $r + 1$ Expensive Terminals:**
   - Suppose for contradiction (or to bound $c$) that Greedy picks $r$ edges of cost $\ge c$. Each such edge was added when some terminal arrived; including the very first terminal $t_1$, there is a set $S$ of **$r + 1$ terminals** such that every terminal in $S$ (when it arrived) was at distance $\ge c$ from all previously arrived terminals in $S$!
   - By the **Triangle Inequality**, the shortest-path distance along the Eulerian tour $C$ between any two consecutive terminals of $S$ along $C$ is at least $c$.
   - Since $r + 1$ terminals partition the closed tour $C$ into $r + 1$ edge-disjoint segments (or using $r$ terminals / segments), the sum of their tour segments is at most $c(C) = 2 \cdot \text{OPT}$.
   - Therefore, the minimum distance between two of these terminals is at most $\frac{2 \cdot \text{OPT}}{r}$, which means at least one of the $r$ terminals in question arrived *after* its neighbor along $C$ and had an available connection option of cost $\le \frac{2 \cdot \text{OPT}}{r}$! $\blacksquare$

## 4. Segue: Online Bipartite Matching & Display Advertising

- **Setup:** Left vertices $L$ (**advertisers** with daily impression budgets/targeting contracts) are known up front; right vertices $R$ (**search queries / page views / "eyeballs"**) arrive online one at a time along with their incident compatible edges to $L$.
- **Decision:** When $w \in R$ arrives, immediately match $w$ to at most one available neighbor $v \in L$.
- **Why Naive Greedy Gets Stuck at $\frac{1}{2}$:** In the 4-node Z-graph ($v_1, v_2 \in L$; $w_1$ connects to $\{v_1, v_2\}$, then $w_2$ connects only to $\{v_1\}$), if Greedy matches $(w_1, v_1)$, then $w_2$ arrives with its only neighbor $v_1$ already taken ($|M| = 1$ vs. $\text{OPT} = 2$). Lecture 14 shows how to achieve $1 - \frac{1}{e} \approx 63.2\%$!
