## 1. Executive Summary

This lecture begins Part IV of the course on **Coping with NP-Hardness via Approximation Algorithms**—designing worst-case polynomial-time algorithms that guarantee solutions within a provable factor $\alpha$ of the optimal solution $\text{OPT}$ on every input instance:
1. **Strategies for Coping with NP-Hardness:** Exploiting special structure (e.g., Bipartite Vertex Cover), beating brute-force exponential search (Lecture 19), or **Approximation Algorithms** (relaxing exactness while insisting on worst-case polynomial time).
2. **Offline Metric Steiner Tree ($2$-Approximation via the MST Heuristic):** Unlike the online setting where any algorithm suffers an $\Omega(\log k)$ competitive ratio (Lecture 13), in the offline setting simply computing a **Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) on the terminals $R$** in the metric completion guarantees $\text{Cost}(\text{MST}(R)) \le 2 \cdot \text{OPT}$!
3. **Maximum Coverage Problem ($\left(1 - \frac{1}{e}\right)$-Approximation via Greedy):** Given $m$ subsets $S_1, \dots, S_m \subseteq U$ and a budget $k$, greedily picking the set covering the most **newly uncovered elements** in each of $k$ steps achieves at least $\left(1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^k\right) \text{OPT} \ge \left(1 - \frac{1}{e}\right) \text{OPT} \approx 63.2\%$ (which is optimal unless $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{NP}$).
4. **Influence Maximization in Social Networks (Kempe, Kleinberg, Tardos 2003):** Selecting $k$ seed nodes in the Independent Cascade model reduces to a stochastic superposition of Maximum Coverage instances, so the same Greedy algorithm achieves a $\left(1 - \frac{1}{e}\right)$-approximation!

## 2. Offline Metric Steiner Tree: The $2$-Approximation MST Heuristic

### 2.1 Problem Recap & The MST Heuristic
Given an undirected complete graph $G = (V, E)$ with edge costs $c_e \ge 0$ satisfying the **Triangle Inequality** ($c_{uw} \le c_{uv} + c_{vw}$) and a subset of **terminals** $R = \{t_1, \dots, t_k\} \subseteq V$:
- **Goal:** Find a minimum-cost tree $T^* \subseteq E$ spanning $R$ (possibly using optional helper "Steiner" nodes in $V \setminus R$).
- **The MST Heuristic:** Completely ignore the non-terminal vertices $V \setminus R$, and simply compute a **Minimum Spanning Tree (MST)** of the induced subgraph on $R$ (via Prim's or Kruskal's algorithm in polynomial time)!

```text
   Wheel Graph Tight Example:               MST Heuristic on Terminals R = {t1..tk}:
        (t1) ---2--- (t2)                        (t1) ===2=== (t2)
          \    |1    /                                         ||
           1  (s)   1                                          2   Cost(MST) = 2(k-1)
            \  |1  /                                           ||  Cost(OPT) = k
             (t3)                                (t4) ===2=== (t3) Ratio -> 2 as k -> ∞!
```

### 2.2 One-Shot Proof That $\text{Cost}(\text{MST}(R)) \le 2 \cdot \text{OPT}$
1. **Double the Optimal Steiner Tree $T^*$:** Let $T^*$ be the optimal Steiner tree (cost $\text{OPT}$), and form multigraph $H$ by taking two copies of every edge in $T^*$. Then $c(H) = 2 \cdot \text{OPT}$, and every vertex in $H$ has even degree, so $H$ has an **Eulerian Tour $C$** of cost $2 \cdot \text{OPT}$ that visits every terminal in $R$.
2. **Shortcut $C$ to a Simple Cycle $C'$ on $R$:** Walk along $C$, skipping any non-terminal vertices and any previously visited terminals by taking direct shortcut edges between consecutive first visits of terminals in $R$. By the **Triangle Inequality**, shortcutting never increases cost:
   $$c(C') \le c(C) = 2 \cdot \text{OPT}$$
3. **Delete One Edge of $C'$ to Get a Spanning Tree of $R$:** Deleting any edge from the simple cycle $C'$ yields a spanning tree $T'$ of $R$ with $c(T') \le c(C') \le 2 \cdot \text{OPT}$. Since our algorithm outputs the **Minimum** Spanning Tree of $R$:
   $$\text{Cost}(\text{MST}(R)) \le c(T') \le 2 \cdot \text{OPT} \quad \blacksquare$$

## 3. The Maximum Coverage Problem & The Greedy $\left(1 - \frac{1}{e}\right)$ Guarantee

### 3.1 Problem Definition
- **Input:** Ground universe $U$, a collection of $m$ subsets $S_1, \dots, S_m \subseteq U$, and an integer budget $k \ge 1$.
- **Goal:** Choose at most $k$ subsets $I \subseteq \{1, \dots, m\}$ ($|I| \le k$) to maximize the size of their union $\left|\bigcup_{i \in I} S_i\right|$.

### 3.2 The Greedy Coverage Algorithm
```text
GREEDY-MAX-COVERAGE(Subsets S_1..S_m, Budget k):
    Covered = ∅
    for i = 1, 2, ..., k:
        Pick set A_i in {S_1..S_m} maximizing |A_i \ Covered|   // most newly covered elements
        Covered = Covered U A_i
    return {A_1, ..., A_k}
```

### 3.3 Why Greedy Is Not Optimal: Tight Bad Examples ($k = 2$ and $k = 3$)

```text
  Ground Universe U (partitioned into k = 3 optimal disjoint columns S1, S2, S3 of size 1/3 each):
  +-------------+-------------+-------------+
  |     S1      |     S2      |     S3      |
  |=============|=============|=============|
  |         S4 (size 1/3 + ε)               |  <-- Step 1: Greedy is tricked into picking S4!
  |-------------|-------------|-------------|
  |         S5 (size 2/9 + ε)               |  <-- Step 2: Greedy is tricked into picking S5!
  |             |             |             |
  |  (1/3)*2/3  |  (1/3)*2/3  |  (1/3)*2/3  |  <-- Step 3: Greedy picks S1 (adds 4/27).
  +-------------+-------------+-------------+      Total Greedy = 1 - (2/3)^3 = 19/27 ≈ 70.4%!
```

In general, for budget $k$, this construction tricks Greedy into leaving a $\left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^k \to \frac{1}{e}$ fraction of the universe uncovered!

### 3.4 Proof That Greedy Achieves $\left(1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^k\right) \text{OPT} \ge \left(1 - \frac{1}{e}\right) \text{OPT}$
Let $f(i) = \left|\bigcup_{j=1}^i A_j\right|$ be the total number of elements covered after iteration $i$ (with $f(0) = 0$).

> **Key Lemma (Diminishing Returns / Remaining Gap):** In every iteration $i \in \{1, \dots, k\}$, the number of newly covered elements is at least $\frac{1}{k}$ of the remaining gap to $\text{OPT}$:
> $$f(i) - f(i - 1) \ge \frac{\text{OPT} - f(i - 1)}{k}$$

**Proof of Key Lemma:**
- The optimal solution covers $\text{OPT}$ elements using $k$ sets $S_{j_1}^*, \dots, S_{j_k}^*$.
- Out of those $\text{OPT}$ elements, at most $f(i - 1)$ are already covered by $A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{i-1}$.
- Therefore, the $k$ optimal sets $S_{j_1}^*, \dots, S_{j_k}^*$ together cover **at least $\text{OPT} - f(i - 1)$ currently uncovered elements**.
- By the Pigeonhole Principle (averaging over the $k$ sets), at least one of the $k$ optimal sets covers $\ge \frac{\text{OPT} - f(i - 1)}{k}$ newly uncovered elements. Since Greedy picks the set maximizing newly covered elements, $f(i) - f(i - 1) \ge \frac{\text{OPT} - f(i - 1)}{k}$. $\blacksquare$

**Unrolling the Recurrence:**
Subtracting both sides from $\text{OPT}$ shows that the **uncovered gap $\text{OPT} - f(i)$ shrinks by a factor of at least $\left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)$ every step**:
$$\text{OPT} - f(i) \le \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)\big(\text{OPT} - f(i - 1)\big) \implies \text{OPT} - f(k) \le \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^k \text{OPT} \le e^{-1} \text{OPT}$$
Rearranging gives:
$$f(k) \ge \left(1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right)^k\right) \text{OPT} \ge \left(1 - \frac{1}{e}\right) \text{OPT} \approx 0.6321 \cdot \text{OPT} \quad \blacksquare$$

## 4. Influence Maximization in Social Networks (Independent Cascade Model)

- **Model (Kempe, Kleinberg, Tardos 2003):** Given a directed social network $G = (V, E)$ and transmission probability $p \in [0, 1]$, activating an initial seed set $S \subseteq V$ ($|S| \le k$) triggers a cascade where each newly active node $v$ independently activates each out-neighbor $w$ with probability $p$.
- **Live-Edge Graph Equivalence:** Flipping all edge coins $(v, w)$ independently up front with probability $p$ yields a random subgraph $G_{\text{live}}$; the set of nodes ultimately activated by seed set $S$ is simply the union of reachable sets $\bigcup_{v \in S} R_{G_{\text{live}}}(v)$—which is **literally a Maximum Coverage instance**!
- Because a non-negative linear combination (expectation) of coverage functions preserves the diminishing-returns (**submodularity**) property, the Greedy Influence Maximization algorithm also achieves a $\left(1 - \frac{1}{e}\right)$-approximation!
